Methodology: The Information Engine

Undercurrent — Methodology: The Information Engine

Version 0.2 — 2026-11-10. This document is the heart of the project and is intended to be published. Every automated run reads it at start.

0. Design stance

The engine is built to behave like a good intelligence analyst rather than a news editor: curious, divergent in search, convergent in judgement, explicit about uncertainty, and accountable for every call it makes. Three commitments shape everything below:

  • The unit of analysis is the claim, not the article.
  • Weight comes from independent origins and track record, never from repetition or prestige alone.
  • Every judgement leaves a trace that can be scored later.

1. The claim model

Every piece of information the engine handles is decomposed into atomic, verifiable claims. A claim record contains:

Field Meaning
id Stable identifier (C-YYYYMMDD-nnn)
text One sentence, verifiable, no interpretation
type fact · leak · rumor · sentiment · forecast · opinion
domains One or more of the six domains
entities Countries, companies, assets, people, technologies, institutions
first_seen Source and date of earliest appearance found
origin The root source after citation tracing (§3.2)
sources[] All sources carrying the claim, each with tier and date
independent_origins Count of mutually independent origins
status unverified · corroborated · confirmed · disputed · denied · falsified · resolved
confidence 0–1 (§3.4)
materiality 0–1 (§6.1)
horizon When effects would materialise: days · weeks · months · years
resolution For leaks/rumors/forecasts: criterion + expected date
storyline Link to the ongoing narrative it belongs to (§7)

Types are not interchangeable. A sentiment claim (“retail forums turned sharply negative on X”) is a measurement of a crowd, never evidence about X itself. An opinion is attributed and never promoted to fact. A forecast always carries a probability.

2. Source registry

2.1 Tiers

Tier Description Examples
P — Primary Original documents and data Regulatory filings, central bank releases, court dockets, official statistics, patents, company statements, earnings calls, legislation texts, scientific papers, job postings, shipping and customs data
A — Authoritative Established general and financial media with editorial standards Wire services, major dailies, major financial press, public broadcasters
S — Specialist Domain media and expert publications Trade journals, sector newsletters, think tanks, academic blogs, research houses
U — Underground Communities and individuals where expertise and leaks surface first Technical forums, subreddits, Hacker News, Discord/Telegram channels, Substack writers, GitHub activity, supply-chain insiders, hobbyist teardown communities, regional-language local press

The tiers describe provenance, not value. Tier U is where the engine expects to find the earliest signals; it is also where manipulation and noise are highest. The registry exists to tell the two apart over time.

2.2 Attributes (per source, per domain where applicable)

Attribute Scale Initial prior How it moves
Reliability R 0–1 P 0.90 · A 0.75 · S 0.65 · U 0.35 ±0.05 per claim resolved true/false, bounded [0.05, 0.98]; decays toward tier prior at 2% per month without data
Latency L median days ahead of first Tier A coverage 0 Measured on each claim later confirmed by Tier A; positive means the source leads
Domain competence per domain, 0–1 tier prior Same update rule, scoped per domain
Agenda / incentives free text + flag — Set on registration; reviewed monthly (ownership, advertising, engagement incentives, political alignment, financial positions)
Independence cluster cluster id — Sources that share ownership, a common feed or a habit of relaying each other without attribution belong to one cluster

A source with low R and high L is a valuable early-warning source: its claims enter the Rumor Ledger quickly but are published only as weak signals until corroborated.

2.3 Registration and growth

The registry starts with a seed list per domain and tier and grows by discovery: every run records sources encountered that are not in the registry, with a provisional tier. A source is promoted from provisional to registered once it has carried at least three claims whose outcome was later resolved. Tier U discovery is deliberate: each run reserves a share of its search budget for exploring communities adjacent to the week’s storylines.

3. Weighting and corroboration

3.1 The core rule

Confidence in a claim grows with the number of independent origins and with the reliability of those origins, not with the number of outlets repeating it.

3.2 Citation tracing

Before counting sources, the engine traces each report of a claim to its origin: who said it first, and did later outlets add independent confirmation or merely relay it? A relay is identified by explicit attribution (“according to X”), by near-identical wording or timing, or by membership in the same independence cluster. Relays are recorded in sources[] but contribute nothing to independent_origins.

3.3 Evidence hierarchy

  1. A primary document confirming the claim → status confirmed, confidence ≥ 0.9 regardless of other sources.
  2. Two or more independent Tier A/S origins with consistent detail → corroborated.
  3. One Tier A/S origin, or several Tier U origins from different clusters → corroborated (weak).
  4. One Tier U origin → unverified; enters the Rumor Ledger.

Official denials move the status to denied but do not falsify: the denial is itself a claim with its own origin reliability. Falsification requires evidence incompatible with the claim.

3.4 Confidence computation (indicative)

Start from the reliability of the best origin. Add diminishing increments for each additional independent origin (second origin +0.10, third +0.05, subsequent +0.02), weighted by their reliability. Subtract for: internal inconsistency between versions (−0.10), active denial from a credible primary party (−0.15), presence of manipulation red flags (−0.10 to −0.30). Clamp to [0.02, 0.98]. The number is published with its reasoning, never alone.

3.5 Manipulation red flags

  • Anonymous sourcing from an account or outlet with no track record.
  • Coordinated timing across unrelated accounts; copy-paste phrasing.
  • Claims aligned with a visible financial position (pump, short campaign) or with engagement incentives.
  • Precision without provenance (“exactly 40% cut”) in a domain where such precision is implausible.
  • Screenshots or documents without metadata and without a second holder.

Flags do not block publication; they lower confidence and are stated in the notes.

4. The underground radar

This is the engine’s distinctive capability. The premise: material information often surfaces first in sub-threshold spaces — technical forums, supply-chain chatter, regulatory dockets, hiring patterns, code repositories, hobbyist communities — mixed with error and manipulation. The radar’s job is to find it early and publish it responsibly.

4.1 Detection methods

Method What it looks for
Novelty New entities, terms, part numbers, project names, drug codes, bill numbers appearing in expert communities before mainstream
Velocity Sudden growth in discussion volume on a narrow topic within a competent community
Expert density Signals from communities where verified practitioners are over-represented (engineers, clinicians, logistics operators, lawyers)
Peripheral evidence Job postings, patents, procurement notices, lobbying registers, customs data, GitHub commits, app teardowns, local press in the relevant language
Cross-community spread The same claim emerging independently in two unrelated communities
Silence Expected announcements that do not occur; sudden deletions

4.2 The Rumor Ledger

Every detected weak signal is recorded with: claim, origin, detection method, domain, entities, estimated probability of being substantially true, resolution criterion, expected resolution date, and the market or societal linkage if true.

4.3 Publication rule

Weak signals are published only in the dedicated Undercurrent section of each report, each with its status, probability and what to watch. They never appear in the factual narrative of the domain sections. When a signal is later confirmed, the report says so and credits the lead time; when falsified, the report says so too.

4.4 Learning loop

Each resolution updates the origin’s R and L and the detection method’s hit rate. The Monthly reviews the Rumor Ledger: hit rate by method, by community, by domain; lead time distribution; false-positive patterns. Over time the radar concentrates on the communities that have actually led.

5. Chains of effects

The engine models interconnections as explicit causal chains, not as narrative suggestions.

5.1 Structure

A chain is a sequence of nodes (events or states) joined by edges, each edge specifying:

  • Mechanism — how A produces B (price, policy, logistics, substitution, sentiment, regulation, technology, demographics).
  • Hop confidence — 0–1 for this edge alone.
  • Horizon — expected lag.
  • Granularity at the end node — market · sector · asset class · single name.
  • Falsifier — what observation would break this hop.

5.2 Decay

Chain confidence is the product of hop confidences. Absent specific evidence, each hop beyond the first carries a default multiplier of 0.7; mechanisms with strong historical regularity (e.g., energy price → fertiliser cost) may use 0.85; speculative mechanisms use 0.5. Reports display at most three hops; anything deeper is a research note, not a published chain.

5.3 Synchronous and asynchronous links

  • Synchronous: events of the same week that interact (a sanction and a shipping disruption compounding).
  • Asynchronous: a current event activating a latent condition recorded earlier in a storyline (a drought recorded in spring becoming a harvest shortfall in autumn). Storylines (§7) are what make asynchronous detection possible.

5.4 Presentation

Chains are published in a fixed format: A → B → C, one line per hop with mechanism, confidence and horizon, followed by the end-node implication at its stated granularity and the falsifier. Second- and third-order nodes are always labelled as hypotheses.

6. Materiality and dynamic relevance

6.1 Materiality of a claim

materiality = impact × probability × breadth × proximity

  • Impact: size of consequence if true; market linkage (potential repricing, capital flows, policy shifts) weighs most, societal consequence second.
  • Probability: the claim’s confidence.
  • Breadth: how many domains, regions or asset classes it touches.
  • Proximity: nearer horizons weigh more for the Weekly; the Monthly compensates for long horizons.

6.2 The Relevance Register

Depth of treatment per domain and per topic is governed by a register of weights.

Domain weights (set by the owner as quotas, 8 / 6 / 5 / 4 / 3 / 2 out of 28; sum = 1). A quota governs both the depth of research in the sweep and the space given to the section in the report:

Domain Quota Weight
Economy & Markets 8 0.29
Technology 6 0.21
Geopolitics 5 0.18
Science, Medicine & Ecology 4 0.14
National politics 3 0.11
Culture & Sport 2 0.07

Topic weights inside each domain are dynamic:

  • Boosted when a topic produces verified effects (price moves, policy changes, mainstream pick-up of a signal first flagged here).
  • Boosted by market linkage declared in chains.
  • Decayed with a half-life of six weeks when no consequences materialise.
  • Floored so that no high-profile domain disappears from the Weekly.

6.3 Ex-post effect review

Every month, for each item published with materiality ≥ 0.6: did a measurable effect occur within the stated horizon? Measured by market data, policy events or mainstream coverage. Results adjust topic weights and are reported in the Monthly’s Verified effects section. Domain weights themselves are revised quarterly, by the owner, on the basis of this review.

7. Storylines

A storyline is a persistent record of an unfolding situation (a conflict, a regulatory process, a technology race, a commodity cycle). It holds: summary, key claims, open questions, active chains, forecasts, last update. Storylines give the engine memory across runs, enable asynchronous chain detection, and prevent each issue from re-explaining context. A storyline is closed when inactive for eight weeks and archived with its outcome.

8. Forecasting and calibration

8.1 Rules for a publishable forecast

Every forecast states: the event, the probability (as a number), the resolution date, the resolution criterion (observable, unambiguous), and the reasoning in two or three sentences. Probability words are standardised:

Word Range
remote < 10%
unlikely 10–30%
uncertain 30–50%
plausible 50–70%
likely 70–90%
near-certain > 90%

8.2 The Forecast Ledger

All forecasts are logged, with their origin (engine, or a source’s forecast adopted by the engine), and resolved on their dates. Scoring uses the Brier score; the Monthly publishes calibration by domain and by horizon, and a reliability table (stated probability vs observed frequency).

8.3 Revision discipline

Forecasts may be updated between issues; each update is logged with its trigger. Silent revision is forbidden: the report shows the previous value and the new one.

9. Operational hypotheses and recommendations

Recommendations are published as operational hypotheses, in two places: at the end of the relevant section (short) and in a dedicated summary section (structured). Each carries:

Element Content
Thesis One sentence
Granularity market · sector · asset class · single name
Direction positioning or action implied (overweight / underweight / hedge / watch / avoid / accumulate / reduce)
Horizon weeks · months · years
Confidence 0–1 with the word from §8.1
Invalidation trigger The observation that would retire the hypothesis
What to watch Upcoming data, events, dates

Rules: hypotheses derive from published claims and chains, never from unpublished reasoning; they are identical for all readers (equal access); they are tracked in the ledger and reviewed with the same discipline as forecasts; a standing disclaimer states that they are general analysis, not personalised advice.

10. Quality assurance

A second, independent pass — a separate Claude instance that has not produced the draft — checks every issue before publication:

  1. Citation integrity: every numbered note resolves to a real, reachable source, dated, that actually supports the sentence it is attached to.
  2. No orphan facts: no factual sentence without a note.
  3. Type discipline: rumors, sentiment, opinions and forecasts are labelled and appear in their proper sections.
  4. Uncertainty language: probability words match the stated numbers; no unqualified predictions.
  5. Chain format: every chain has mechanisms, hop confidences, horizon, granularity, falsifier.
  6. Forecast and hypothesis completeness: all fields present; ledger entries created.
  7. Conflict disclosure: where sources disagree, the note states both positions and the engine’s weighting.
  8. Copyright: paraphrase by default; quotations rare and short; no reproduction of articles.
  9. Tone: analytic, plain, no hype, no partisan framing; political topics treated even-handedly.
  10. Structure and length within specification.

Failures are returned to the drafting pass with the failing items. An issue that cannot pass is published late with a note, never published unchecked.

11. Notes and citations format

Notes are numbered consecutively within each section and placed at the end of that section:

[n] Source name, Tier, date — status of the claim (confirmed / corroborated / unverified / disputed). Conflict or caveat if any.

Where sources conflict, one note carries both positions and the reason for the weighting.

12. Ethics and limits

  • Political and geopolitical topics are reported from multiple perspectives; the engine describes positions and consequences, it does not endorse.
  • Health and science claims follow the evidence hierarchy strictly; preprints and press releases are labelled as such.
  • Market-moving rumors about individuals are not published without corroboration.
  • The engine states what it could not verify rather than omitting it silently.

Undercurrent is independent analysis for general information. Operational hypotheses express the authors' reading of public information at the date of publication, with the stated probabilities and invalidation triggers; they are not personalised investment, legal or medical advice and do not consider any reader's circumstances. Sources, methodology and the full record of past calls are public.
